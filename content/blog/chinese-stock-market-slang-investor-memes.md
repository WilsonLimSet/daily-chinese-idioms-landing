Chinese retail investors have built one of the most vivid slang vocabularies on the internet. Almost none of it is financial jargon. It's food, vehicles, farming, and one very specific rooftop.

If you read Chinese stock forums — 东方财富股吧, 雪球 (Xueqiu), the finance side of Weibo — this is the vocabulary that actually appears, and none of it will be in your textbook.

---

## The Quick Version

Chinese investor slang runs on four metaphor families:

1. **Farming** — you are a vegetable, and someone harvests you (韭菜)
2. **Food** — winning is meat, losing is noodles (吃肉 / 吃面)
3. **Vehicles** — a position is a car you board and exit (上车 / 下车)
4. **Disaster humor** — the rooftop, the meat-cutting, the standing guard (天台见 / 割肉 / 站岗)

Once you see the families, the individual terms stop needing memorization.

---

## 1. The Farming Family — You Are the Crop

### 韭菜 (jiǔ cài) — "leeks"

Retail investors. Specifically: retail investors as *food*.

Chinese chives (韭菜) regrow after you cut them, which is the entire joke. You get harvested, you recover, you come back, you get harvested again. It is the most-used word in Chinese retail-investing culture and it is always self-aware — people call *themselves* 韭菜 constantly.

- **老韭菜** — "old leek," a veteran who has been cut many times
- **新韭菜** — "new leek," fresh money that doesn't know yet

### 割韭菜 (gē jiǔ cài) — "harvesting the leeks"

The act: institutions, pump-and-dump operators, or a crypto project extracting money from retail. Now used far outside finance for any exploitative business model — overpriced courses, scam apps, bad IPOs.

> 别被割韭菜了。*"Don't let yourself get harvested."*

## 2. The Food Family — Meat or Noodles

### 吃肉 (chī ròu) — "eating meat"

Your position is up. You're making money.

### 吃面 (chī miàn) — "eating noodles"

Your position is down. Plain noodles are what you eat when you can't afford meat, and that's the whole metaphor.

### 关灯吃面 (guān dēng chī miàn) — "eating noodles with the lights off"

The full, canonical form — and one of the best-documented memes in Chinese internet history.

On **15 December 2011**, Chongqing Brewery (重庆啤酒) collapsed from roughly 80 yuan to 20 after its hepatitis-B vaccine trial showed no significant therapeutic effect. A shareholder posted on the East Money forum under the title 一边吃，一边哭 ("eating and crying"):

> 今天回到家，煮了点面吃，一边吃面一边哭，泪水滴落在碗里，没有开灯。
>
> *"Got home today, cooked some noodles, cried while I ate, tears fell in the bowl, never turned the light on."*

One sentence, no self-pity, total devastation. It became permanent vocabulary and has long since escaped finance — you'll see it used for any quiet private misery.

## 3. The Vehicle Family — Positions Are Rides

### 上车 (shàng chē) — "board the car"

Enter a position. Buy in.

### 下车 (xià chē) — "get off"

Exit a position. Sell.

### 换车 (huàn chē) — "change cars"

Sell one holding to buy another.

### 坐轿 (zuò jiào) — "riding the sedan chair"

You bought early and are now being carried up by everyone buying after you. The good seat.

### 抬轿 (tái jiào) — "lifting the sedan chair"

You bought *after* the rise started, so you're the one doing the carrying. The bad seat. The pair is one of the sharpest bits of Chinese market vocabulary — it encodes an entire theory of who profits from whom.

## 4. The Disaster Family

### 天台见 (tiān tái jiàn) — "see you on the roof"

Catastrophic loss, said as a joke. The rooftop is where you'd go to jump, so the phrase means "I'm ruined" delivered with a laugh. Standard reply: **天台已满，请排队** — *"the roof is full, please queue."*

It came out of the 2014 World Cup betting wave before migrating into stock forums, and it surged again during the July 2026 tech selloff.

[Full deep dive on 天台见 →](/blog/tian-tai-jian-meaning-chinese-slang-see-you-on-the-roof)

### 套牢 (tào láo) — "tied up / trapped"

You bought, it fell, and now you're stuck holding because selling would lock in the loss. The single most useful term on this list — it names a specific, universal psychological state.

### 站岗 (zhàn gǎng) — "standing guard"

The vivid version of 套牢: you bought at the peak and now you're posted up there indefinitely, keeping watch over a position that isn't coming back. Often **山顶站岗** — "standing guard on the mountaintop."

### 割肉 (gē ròu) — "cutting flesh"

Selling at a loss. Taking the pain deliberately to stop the bleeding. English says "cut your losses"; Chinese makes it considerably more graphic.

## 5. The Practical Terms

These aren't memes — they're the ordinary working vocabulary you need to follow a conversation.

### 抄底 (chāo dǐ) — "scooping the bottom"

Buying the dip at what you believe is the low.

### 抄在半山腰 (chāo zài bàn shān yāo) — "scooping halfway up the mountain"

Trying to buy the bottom and discovering it wasn't the bottom. The self-deprecating companion to 抄底, and much more commonly deserved.

### 大盘 (dà pán) — "the big board"

The overall market / the index. 大盘跌了 = "the market's down."

### 牛市 / 熊市 (niú shì / xióng shì)

Bull market / bear market — a direct calque from English, and completely standard.

### 涨停 / 跌停 (zhǎng tíng / diē tíng)

Limit-up and limit-down. Chinese exchanges halt a stock once it moves ±10% in a day (±20% on ChiNext and the STAR Market), so these are hard daily boundaries, not just descriptions of a big move. This is why Chinese market slang is so extreme about single days — the ceiling is structural.

### 追涨杀跌 (zhuī zhǎng shā diē) — "chasing the rise, killing on the fall"

Buying high out of FOMO and selling low out of panic. The behavior every experienced 韭菜 warns the new ones about, and the one they all do anyway.

## Why the Vocabulary Looks Like This

Two structural facts explain the whole register.

**First, the A-share market is unusually retail-heavy.** Individual investors drive a very large share of turnover, far more than in most developed markets. So the culture around it is popular culture, not professional culture — it develops on forums and in group chats, and it sounds like it.

**Second, the daily price limits compress everything.** When a stock can only move 10% before halting, a limit-down day is a hard, visible, collective event. Everyone hits the wall at the same moment. That produces exactly the humor you'd expect: shared, immediate, and loudly fatalistic — which is why the standard answer to "see you on the roof" is "get in line."

It is worth noticing that the funniest terms are all self-directed. Nobody calls someone *else* a 韭菜 as an insult nearly as often as they call themselves one. The vocabulary is a coping mechanism first and a description second — closer in spirit to [躺平](/slang/tang-ping) and [摆烂](/slang/bai-lan) than to anything in a finance textbook.
