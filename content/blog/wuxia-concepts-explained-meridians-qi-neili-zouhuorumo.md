**Quick answer:** When a Chinese drama says someone's **meridians were broken** — or that their martial arts were **destroyed** — it isn't a random injury. It's the removal of the character's entire power source, and in the logic of the genre it's closer to **blinding someone permanently** than to breaking a bone.

The system works like this. **气 (qì)** is vital energy. It travels the body through **经脉 (jīngmài)**, the meridians, and is stored in the **丹田 (dāntián)**, a centre below the navel. Cultivated over years, it becomes **内力 (nèilì)**, internal force — the thing that lets wuxia characters fly, strike from a distance, and heal. **Sever the meridians and the energy has nowhere to travel**: the practitioner is not just injured but *disarmed for life*, usually irreversibly.

Here's the full vocabulary.

---

## 气 (qì) — the substrate

**气** is the base unit of the entire system: vital energy, breath, the animating force of a living body.

Crucially, **this is not a fantasy invention.** 气 is a foundational concept of traditional Chinese medicine, of Daoist practice, and of qigong (**气功**, literally "qi work"), all of which long predate wuxia fiction. The novels didn't make it up; they **weaponized** something already in the culture.

That's why the system feels internally consistent in a way invented magic often doesn't. It's borrowing a real framework.

## 经脉 (jīngmài) — the meridians

The channels 气 travels through. In Chinese medicine, the meridian network is the map that acupuncture works on — points along the channels where the flow can be influenced.

Wuxia takes that map and makes it **structural**: if 气 moves through channels, then the channels are infrastructure, and infrastructure can be destroyed.

Hence the line English viewers keep hitting: **"break her meridians."** In Chinese that's typically **挑断经脉** (sever) or **震碎经脉** (shatter). It means: *destroy the pathways so the energy can never be used again.*

Two things follow, and they explain why the moment always lands so heavily:

1. **It is usually permanent.** The genre treats meridian destruction as near-unfixable, barring a legendary remedy or an extraordinary master.
2. **It is a punishment, not a fight outcome.** It is generally done deliberately, to someone already defeated, as an act of erasure. It's the wuxia equivalent of stripping a knight of rank and hamstringing them on the way out.

**任督二脉** (*rèn dū èr mài*) — the Conception and Governing vessels, the two most important channels — deserve a mention: "opening the 任督二脉" is the standard phrase for a breakthrough that unlocks a practitioner's full potential. It's so common that modern Chinese uses it metaphorically for any conceptual breakthrough at work.

## 丹田 (dāntián) — the reservoir

The **dantian**, the "cinnabar field," is where cultivated energy is stored — conventionally located just below the navel. Real practice locates it the same way; **气沉丹田** ("sink the qi to the dantian") is standard instruction in qigong, martial arts and even classical singing.

In fiction it functions as the character's **battery**. Destroying it is a tier above destroying meridians — the reservoir itself, not just the plumbing.

## 内力 / 内功 (nèilì / nèigōng) — internal force

**内力** is accumulated, refined 气 — the "internal force" that separates a wuxia master from a person who is merely good at hitting.

The distinction the genre cares most about:

- **外功** (*wàigōng*), "external skill" — technique, speed, muscle, weapons
- **内功** (*nèigōng*), "internal skill" — cultivated energy, developed over decades

External skill has a ceiling and fades with age. **Internal skill compounds**, which is why the terrifying figure in a wuxia story is so often a frail-looking old person. It also explains a recurring plot device: a master **transferring** their internal force to a student, sacrificing a lifetime of cultivation in one act.

## 走火入魔 (zǒu huǒ rù mó) — qi deviation

**"Fire runs wild, the demon enters."**

The system's built-in failure mode. Cultivate wrongly — too fast, with a flawed manual, or while emotionally unstable — and the energy goes out of control. Symptoms in fiction: blood from the mouth, white or red hair, madness, sometimes death.

It is the genre's most important safety rule, and it's what makes cultivation *dramatic* rather than merely grindy: **going faster can destroy you.** A character tempted to rush their training is a character flirting with 走火入魔.

The phrase has fully escaped fiction. In ordinary modern Chinese, **走火入魔** means becoming dangerously obsessed with anything — a hobby, a theory, a diet.

## 废武功 (fèi wǔgōng) — destroying someone's martial arts

**废** means to abolish, to render useless. **废武功** is the umbrella term for stripping a martial artist of their ability, whether by severing meridians, destroying the dantian, or force-draining their 内力.

Understand this as **social death**. In a wuxia world, martial ability is livelihood, rank, and identity all at once. A character who is 废-ed loses their profession, their standing in the sect hierarchy, and their capacity to protect anyone. Dramas frequently treat it as worse than being killed, and characters often say so.

## Why the system is good storytelling

Worth noticing what this framework buys a writer, because it's the reason wuxia works:

- **Power is earned over time**, so age and discipline mean something
- **Power can be taken away**, so stakes survive even for the strongest character
- **Power has a failure mode** (走火入魔), so ambition is genuinely risky
- **Power is transferable**, so sacrifice is possible

Compare a system where a hero simply *is* strong. This one gives you loss, patience, temptation and sacrifice as native mechanics.

## The idioms that come from cultivation

- **[炉火纯青](/blog/lu-huo-chun-qing)** (*lú huǒ chún qīng*) — "the furnace fire burns pure blue." From Daoist alchemy: the flame's colour when the elixir is perfected. Means total mastery.
- **[登峰造极](/blog/deng-feng-zao-ji)** (*dēng fēng zào jí*) — "climb the peak, reach the summit." The highest attainable level.
- **[出神入化](/blog/chu-shen-ru-hua)** (*chū shén rù huà*) — "leaving the spirit, entering transformation." Skill so refined it stops looking like technique.
- **[返老还童](/blog/fan-lao-huan-tong)** (*fǎn lǎo huán tóng*) — "reverse the aging, return to childhood." A Daoist immortality goal, and a real wuxia plot point for masters of extreme cultivation.

## Frequently asked

**What does "breaking someone's meridians" mean?**
Destroying the channels 气 flows through, permanently removing their ability to use martial arts. It's an act of erasure, usually inflicted on someone already defeated.

**Can broken meridians be healed?**
Usually not. The genre treats it as near-permanent unless a legendary medicine or an exceptional master intervenes.

**What is 内力?**
Internal force — cultivated, refined qi. It's what separates a master from someone who's merely fast and strong.

**What is 走火入魔?**
Qi deviation — cultivating wrongly and losing control of your own energy. In modern Chinese it also means being dangerously obsessed with anything.

**Is qi a real belief?**
气 is a foundational concept in traditional Chinese medicine and Daoist practice, long predating wuxia fiction. The novels dramatize an existing framework rather than inventing one.

**What's the difference between 内功 and 外功?**
内功 is internal cultivation (energy, compounding with age); 外功 is external technique (speed, strength, weapons).

**What does 江湖 mean?**
"Rivers and lakes" — the martial world, a society running on its own code outside official authority.
