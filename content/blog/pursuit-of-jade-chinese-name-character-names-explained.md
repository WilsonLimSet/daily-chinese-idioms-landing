**Quick answer:** *Pursuit of Jade*'s Chinese name is **逐玉** (*Zhú Yù*) — literally **"chasing jade."** The English title is a fair translation but loses the drama's central pun: the heroine is named **樊长玉 (Fán Chángyù)**, and the last character of her name **is 玉, jade**. The title isn't only about a precious stone or a moral ideal. It names *her*. Every faction in the drama is, in some sense, pursuing Fan Changyu — for her bloodline, her usefulness, or her hand — and the title says so in two characters.

Below: what each major character's name means, and why several of them are jokes at their owner's expense.

---

## 逐玉 (Zhú Yù) — the title

**逐** (*zhú*) is a hard verb. Not "seek" or "admire" — **to chase, to pursue, to drive after.** It's the 逐 of 追逐 (chasing) and 角逐 (contending for). There is effort and rivalry baked into it.

**玉** (*yù*) is jade — which in Chinese carries a moral weight the English word simply doesn't have. Jade stands for integrity, incorruptibility, and the cultivated person. (We took that apart properly in [Why Jade Symbolism Matters](/blog/pursuit-of-jade-why-jade-symbolism-matters-chinese-culture).)

So 逐玉 reads three ways at once, all of them intended:

1. **Chasing the stone** — the literal treasure-and-power plot
2. **Chasing virtue** — Xie Zheng pursuing a clean name for his family
3. **Chasing Changyu** — the woman whose name ends in 玉

English can carry the first two. It cannot carry the third, which is why the Chinese title is better.

## 樊长玉 (Fán Chángyù) — the butcher's daughter

- **樊 (Fán)** — the surname. Note the character: 樊, not the more common 范. Worth knowing if you're searching Chinese sources.
- **长 (cháng)** — long, lasting, enduring. Read as *zhǎng* it means "to grow."
- **玉 (yù)** — jade.

**"Enduring jade," or "jade that grows."**

For a girl raised in hiding as a butcher's daughter, being named for the stone that signifies nobility of character is either a cruel joke or a promise, and the drama plays it as a promise. She is jade the whole time — the world just has her filed under meat.

The double reading of 长 is doing real work. *Cháng* (lasting) describes what she is; *zhǎng* (growing) describes what she does across forty episodes, ending as the **Flower-Crowned General (簪花将军, zān huā jiāng jūn)**.

## 谢征 (Xiè Zhēng) — the marquis

- **谢 (Xiè)** — the surname. As an ordinary word it means "to thank" and also "to wither" — a fallen noble house carrying a surname that can mean decline is not an accident.
- **征 (zhēng)** — **to campaign, to march on, to levy.** A purely military character: 出征 (to set out on campaign), 远征 (expedition).

His name is essentially **"the campaign."** He is defined by the war he is waging, and he spends sixteen years as a refugee under the alias **颜征 (Yán Zhēng)** — swapping only the surname, keeping 征. He'll give up his family name to survive. He will not give up the campaign.

## 樊大贵 (Fán Dàguì) — the father

**大贵** means **"great nobility"** or "greatly honored." He is a pig butcher in a provincial town.

The joke lands only after the reveal: he is the son of **General Wei Qilin**, and he traded a genuinely noble lineage for an apron and a cleaver to keep his daughters alive. The name isn't ironic. It's accurate, and only the audience is in on it.

## 魏麒麟 (Wèi Qílín) — the executed general

The most loaded name in the drama.

**麒麟** is the **qilin** — the benevolent composite beast of Chinese myth, often called the "Chinese unicorn." What matters is what the qilin *signifies*: it is an omen-beast whose appearance marks the birth or death of a sage. It is famously gentle — legend says it walks without crushing a blade of grass. **And it punishes only the wicked.** ([Full entry on the qilin →](/mythology/qilin))

Naming the framed, scapegoated, executed general **Wei Qilin** is the single sharpest piece of writing in the show. A creature that harms only the wicked, destroyed by the state as a traitor. The name is the verdict on the regime, embedded in a character who is dead before episode 1.

## 长信王 (Chángxìn Wáng) — Prince Changxin

**长信** means **"lasting trust"** or "enduring faith." He is a rebel prince who raises an army against the throne and promises to share power with collaborators he has no intention of honoring.

Chinese period dramas do this constantly — princely titles are aspirational or propagandistic, not descriptive, exactly as real imperial regnal names were. Reading a prince's title as a claim rather than a fact is a useful habit. ([Who is Prince Changxin? →](/blog/pursuit-of-jade-who-is-prince-changxin-wei-qilin-villains-explained))

## Frequently asked

**What is Pursuit of Jade's Chinese name?**
逐玉 (*Zhú Yù*), literally "chasing jade."

**Is it 逐玉 or 追玉?**
逐玉. 追玉 circulates in some English listings but is not the drama's title.

**What does Fan Changyu's name mean?**
樊长玉 — "enduring jade" or "growing jade." The 玉 in her name is the jade of the title.

**Is the drama's title about the heroine?**
Partly, yes — deliberately. It works simultaneously as the treasure plot, the moral ideal, and her name.

**What does Xie Zheng's name mean?**
谢征 — 征 means "to campaign/march." His refugee alias 颜征 changes the surname but keeps 征.

**Why is Wei Qilin named after a mythical beast?**
麒麟 (qilin) is an omen-beast that punishes only the wicked. Naming the wrongly executed general after it is the drama's judgment on the throne that killed him.
