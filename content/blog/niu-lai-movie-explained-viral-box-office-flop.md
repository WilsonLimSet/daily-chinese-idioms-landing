**Quick answer:** *Niu Lai* (**牛来**, *Niú Lái*, released internationally as **Here Comes the Ox**) is a 2026 Chinese animated feature that failed so completely it became a phenomenon. Released **5 August 2026** in 245 cinemas, it earned **¥7,169 — about US$995 — from 236 viewers in its first nine days**, and was down to **4 screens by 14 August**.

Then the number itself went viral. On **14 August** a social-media post about that nine-day box office took off, and audiences started buying tickets **out of curiosity about how bad it could be**. Takings jumped from a few hundred yuan a day to over ¥130,000 in a single day, and the cumulative total has since climbed into the millions.

It was made by **two people** — director **Xin Yumeng** and writer **Sun Lifang**, who are the only listed crew and also voice the characters — at a company that until 2021 was an **interior-decorating firm**.

| Date | What happened |

| 5–13 Aug | Takes **¥7,169** total, from **236 tickets** |

| 14–15 Aug | Single-day takings jump past **¥130,000**; presales pass ¥100,000 |
| Mid-Aug | Cumulative total climbs into the **millions of yuan** |

Roughly 26 people a day nationwide, in a country of 1.4 billion, in 245 cinemas. That is the statistic that broke containment.

## Why it went viral

Not the film. **The gap.**

Two gaps, specifically. The first is between the film's **poster** — an elegant, traditional **ink-wash painting (水墨)** — and the animation behind it, which viewers described as *"disaster-level"* and *"worse than AI-generated."* A poster promising 《大鱼海棠》 and a film delivering something closer to a first-year exercise.

The second gap is the money. **¥7,169** is not a small number in the way a flop is normally small; it's a number you can count in individual human beings. It reads less like a box-office result than like a punchline, and Chinese social media treated it as one.

Once that happened, the incentive inverted. People bought tickets **because** it was bad — to see the thing everyone was posting about. The film's own official account responded with unusual grace, acknowledging it fell *"short of established industry standards"* while thanking people for the attention.

## The title is a pun, and the pun is why it spread

Here's the part that made it irresistible to Chinese internet culture.

**牛 (niú)** is an ox or a cow. But 牛 is also **slang for "awesome"** — the polite short form of 牛逼 (*niúbī*), which is how a Chinese speaker says something is impressive or badass. ([Full breakdown of 牛/牛逼 →](/blog/niu-bi-meaning-chinese-slang-awesome-cool-history))

And **牛市 (niú shì)** is a **bull market**.

So **牛来** — "the ox is coming" — also reads as *"awesome is coming"* and, most usefully for a meme, **"the bull market is coming."** Stock-market posters seized it immediately: a film called *The Bull Is Coming*, taking ¥7,169, became a joke about market optimism meeting reality. (If you want that whole vocabulary — 韭菜, 关灯吃面, 天台见 — we mapped it in [Chinese stock market slang](/blog/chinese-stock-market-slang-investor-memes).)

A title that puns three ways is a title built for sharing, even if nobody involved planned it.

## 黑红 — the concept doing the work

The Chinese internet has a precise word for what happened here: **黑红** (*hēi hóng*), literally **"black-red."**

- **红 (hóng)**, "red," is the standard word for being famous or popular — 网红 (*wǎnghóng*) is an internet celebrity, literally "net-red."
- **黑 (hēi)**, "black," means to be attacked, mocked, or dragged online.

**黑红** is fame that arrives *through* being mocked. The associated saying is **黑红也是红** — **"black-red is still red."** Notoriety counts. Attention is attention.

*Niu Lai* is a near-perfect case study: a film that could not sell tickets on its merits sold them on its infamy, and converted ridicule directly into revenue. Nobody watching is pretending it's good. That was never the transaction.

## What it isn't

Worth stating plainly, because the story invites the wrong lesson.

A researcher quoted in Chinese coverage, Zhang Peng, made the point that curiosity-driven virality **should not be mistaken for a replicable formula** — that "content quality remains the enduring value."

He's right, and the arithmetic backs him. The film needed **nine days of near-total failure** to generate the number that made it interesting. You cannot plan that. Any studio setting out to make a lucrative disaster would simply make a disaster, and the internet, which is very good at detecting effort, would ignore it. What made *Niu Lai* funny is that it was **sincere** — two people who genuinely tried and shipped an 86-minute feature into 245 cinemas.

That sincerity is also why the mockery stayed relatively warm. The tone was rarely cruelty toward the filmmakers; it was astonishment that the system let this reach a screen at all.

## The film itself

For completeness: it follows a young calf and his friendship with a leopard character named **Bao La**, who is rejected by the cattle herd as an outsider. An 86-minute Mandarin-language feature from **Dalian Jingyuan Culture Film and Television Media Co Ltd** — a company registered in 2016 as a decoration and interior-design business that moved into media in **July 2021**.
