**Quick answer:** **Emperor Qi Sheng (齐笙)** is the reigning emperor when *Pursuit of Jade* begins — and the least powerful person in the drama's power structure. He is the **late Emperor Qi Yi's younger surviving son**, installed on a throne that his own father emptied by engineering the Jinzhou massacre to kill his elder brother, Crown Prince Chengde. Chancellor **Wei Yan** put him there because he was manageable. Qi Sheng inherited his father's secret along with the crown, and spends the drama **resisting any reopening of the Jinzhou case** — not because he committed the crime, but because he is standing on it. He grows steadily more paranoid and unstable, **hands the imperial seal to Qi Min under coercion** during the palace coup rather than fight, and is ultimately **forced to abdicate**. He is alive at the end and politically irrelevant.

---

## Where Qi Sheng comes from

The single most important fact about Qi Sheng is that **he was never supposed to be emperor.**

The Jinzhou massacre — the atrocity the entire drama orbits — was not a battlefield failure. It was a purge. The late **Emperor Qi Yi (齐屹)**, paranoid about his own military and about his own dangerously popular son, engineered the abandonment of the Jinzhou garrison specifically to kill **Crown Prince Chengde**, who was visiting the front. The Xie family and General Wei Qilin were collateral damage, framed to make a regicide look like a rout. ([The full villain structure →](/blog/pursuit-of-jade-who-is-prince-changxin-wei-qilin-villains-explained))

That purge left a vacancy. Qi Sheng, the younger surviving son, filled it.

He didn't win the throne. He was **left** it, by a father who murdered his brother to clear the path — and then installed by **Chancellor Wei Yan**, who had every reason to prefer a weak emperor to a strong one.

## Why he protects the cover-up

Here's the thing that makes Qi Sheng genuinely interesting rather than just weak: **he is not guilty of the original crime.** He was not the architect. He may not even have been told the full truth at the time.

But he is *standing on it*. His legitimacy exists only because his brother died at Jinzhou. Reopening the case doesn't just embarrass the previous reign — it retroactively makes his entire kingship the product of a murder. Every year he sits on the throne, the cover-up becomes more load-bearing.

So he resists. Not out of malice, but out of the ordinary self-preservation of a man who cannot afford the answer to a question everyone keeps asking. And the Wei faction's continued protection of him is the drama's **present-tense conspiracy** — distinct from the sixteen-year-old one, and the thing Xie Zheng actually has to fight through.

The drama's judgment is precise: **complicit in the maintenance, not in the crime.**

## What happens to Emperor Qi Sheng?

He unravels.

Across the drama Qi Sheng is portrayed as increasingly suspicious, cornered, and mentally unstable — a man who knows the ground under him is rotten and can't say so to anyone. He suspects his officials, his generals, and eventually everyone.

**The coup.** When **Qi Min** — the dispossessed legitimate heir, working through Prince Changxin's army and Wei Yan's court influence — storms the palace, Qi Sheng does not resist. He **physically hands over the imperial seal under coercion.** For a Chinese period drama, this is about as complete a statement of a ruler's illegitimacy as the genre has: the seal (玉玺) is the concrete embodiment of the Mandate, and he simply gives it away.

**The abdication.** Qi Sheng is forced off the throne. The child **Bao'er (俞宝儿)** is installed as the young emperor, with Xie Zheng briefly serving as **Prince Regent (摄政王)** to stabilize the court before stepping back and refusing the throne himself.

**He survives.** Qi Sheng isn't executed. He ends the drama alive and completely irrelevant — which, in a story where nearly every other conspirator dies, is its own specific verdict. Death would have made him significant.

## Is Qi Sheng the villain?

No — and the drama is careful about this.

*Pursuit of Jade* stacks its villainy in layers, and Qi Sheng isn't any of them. **Prince Changxin** is the public antagonist with the army. **Wei Yan** is the apparent architect. **Emperor Qi Yi** is the real first mover, and he's been dead since before episode 1. **Qi Min** is the dispossessed heir turning all of them into instruments.

Qi Sheng is something the genre attempts less often: **the beneficiary.** He didn't build the crime, he didn't even choose it — he just took what it produced and then spent sixteen years refusing to look at it. That's a more ordinary kind of failure than villainy, and a more recognizable one.

## The idioms that fit him

- **尸位素餐** (*shī wèi sù cān*) — "to occupy the post like a corpse and eat for free." Holding an office while doing none of its work. The classical Chinese phrase for exactly this kind of ruler.
- **名存实亡** (*míng cún shí wáng*) — "the name survives, the substance is dead." An emperor in title with no power in fact.
- **[孤注一掷](/blog/gu-zhu-yi-zhi)** (*gū zhù yī zhì*) — "stake everything on one throw." What he never does. Offered the chance to fight for his throne, he hands over the seal instead.

## Frequently asked

**Who is Emperor Qi Sheng in Pursuit of Jade?**
The reigning emperor at the start of the drama — the late Emperor Qi Yi's younger surviving son, installed after the Jinzhou massacre killed the Crown Prince.

**Is Qi Sheng a villain?**
Not really. He didn't commit the original crime, but he protects the cover-up because his own legitimacy depends on it.

**Does Emperor Qi Sheng die?**
No. He's forced to abdicate and survives, politically irrelevant.

**Why does Qi Sheng give up the imperial seal?**
When Qi Min storms the palace, Qi Sheng surrenders the seal under coercion rather than fight — the drama's clearest statement that he never truly held the throne.

**Who becomes emperor after Qi Sheng?**
Bao'er (俞宝儿), as a child emperor, with Xie Zheng briefly serving as Prince Regent before stepping back.

**How is Qi Sheng related to Prince Changxin?**
Sources describe Changxin as his brother, half-brother, or close cousin — it's inconsistent. What's clear is that Changxin is a senior imperial prince and rebel, not the architect of the original crime.
