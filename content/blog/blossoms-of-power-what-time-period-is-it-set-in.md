**Quick answer:** *Blossoms of Power* (**百花杀**) is **not set in a real dynasty.** It's **架空** (*jiàkōng*) — literally "suspended in mid-air" — a Chinese genre term for a deliberately fictional, un-dated setting that borrows a historical era's look and institutions without claiming to *be* that era. So there's no reign to look up.

But it isn't borrowing at random. The drama's scaffolding is overwhelmingly **Tang**: the title comes from a late-Tang rebel's poem, the court vocabulary and official ranks are Tang-shaped, and the villain archetypes are lifted from named Tang chancellors. The **incense culture** at the heart of the plot, however, is codified **Song**. Knowing which parts come from where is most of what makes the show legible.

## What 架空 actually means

**架空 (jiàkōng)** is one of the most useful words a c-drama viewer can learn. Literally "to suspend in the air" — to build something with no ground under it.

Applied to fiction, it means a setting that is **deliberately, officially not real**: invented dynasty name, invented emperors, invented geography, but furnished with the costumes, ranks, and institutions of a recognizable period.

It is not laziness, and it is not the same as historical inaccuracy. It's a **structural choice**, and in mainland Chinese production it's often a practical one — a fictional dynasty can host palace coups, corrupt chancellors and murdered emperors without a production making claims about actual Chinese history, which is a sensitive thing to dramatize.

So when a viewer asks "what year is this set in?", the honest answer for a 架空 drama is: **the question doesn't apply.** The better question is *what is it furnished with?*

## The Tang scaffolding

Almost everything structural in *Blossoms of Power* points at the **Tang dynasty (618–907)**, and specifically its late, decaying end.

**The title.** 百花杀 is the back half of a couplet by **Huang Chao (黄巢)** — 我花开后百花杀, "after my flower blooms, the hundred flowers die" — from 《不第后赋菊》, written after he failed the imperial examinations. Huang Chao then led the rebellion (875–884) that effectively broke the Tang. A drama that names itself after *that* poem is pointing at the end of a dynasty, by a man who helped end it. ([Full breakdown of the poem →](/blog/blossoms-of-power-title-poem-huang-chao-meaning))

That same poem also gives the drama its central image: **冲天香阵透长安** — "a sky-piercing wave of fragrance pierces Chang'an." A scent that overwhelms a capital. The heroine's entire method is a fragrance that overwhelms a court.

**The villains.** Two of the drama's most-used idioms come attached to real Tang chancellors:

- **笑里藏刀** ("a knife hidden in a smile") attached itself to **Li Yifu (李义府)**, a Tang official so mild in manner and vicious in action that the phrase stuck to him permanently.
- **口蜜腹剑** ("honey in the mouth, a sword in the belly") was coined for **Li Linfu (李林甫)**, the Tang chancellor whose flattery masked ruthless scheming.

The drama's court politics are, in other words, furnished with Tang-specific archetypes of treachery.

**The vocabulary.** The court terms are Tang-shaped throughout — **东宫** (*dōnggōng*, the "Eastern Palace," meaning the Crown Prince's establishment and by extension the Crown Prince himself), and the heroine's rank **昭宁郡主** (*Zhāoníng jùnzhǔ*). Note the rank: **郡主** is a *commandery princess*, not 公主 (*gōngzhǔ*, an emperor's daughter). It's a real, specific tier of imperial nobility, and the distinction matters for reading her standing at court.

## The Song borrowing

The one big element that **isn't** Tang is the thing the whole plot runs on.

**香道** (*xiāng dào*), the "way of incense," is a scholar-elite discipline on the level of tea or calligraphy — and its codification is **Song**. Song literati formalized the **四般闲事** (*sì bān xián shì*), the "four idle arts" a cultivated person was expected to practice, with incense among them.

So the drama's fragrance culture — the connoisseurship, the blending, the idea of scent as a refined art a noblewoman would master — is drawn from a later period than its politics. ([The full incense tradition →](/blog/blossoms-of-power-incense-culture-scent-as-weapon))

This is completely normal for 架空. The setting is a composite: **Tang bones, Song manners.**

## So what should you tell someone who asks?

> *Blossoms of Power* is set in a fictional dynasty, not a real one. It's built on late-Tang political architecture — the title is a Huang Chao poem, the court ranks and villain archetypes are Tang — with Song-dynasty incense culture layered on top. Don't look for a date.

## Why 架空 is worth recognizing generally

Once you know the term, you'll notice how much of the costume-drama shelf is 架空 rather than historical, and it changes how you watch:

- **Don't fact-check the politics.** Nothing is claiming to have happened.
- **Do read the furnishings.** The period a 架空 drama borrows from tells you what it wants to be *about*. Choosing late Tang signals dynastic decay and factional rot before a line of dialogue is spoken.
- **Watch for the mismatches.** They're usually deliberate. Tang politics with Song refinement gives you a court that is simultaneously brutal and exquisite — which is exactly this drama's texture.

Compare: *Key to the Phoenix Heart* is also 架空 (a fictional princedom), while *Pursuit of Jade* invents a "Great Yin" dynasty outright. Different shows, same move.

## The idioms that come with the setting

- **[尔虞我诈](/blog/er-yu-wo-zha)** (*ěr yú wǒ zhà*) — "you deceive, I cheat." Mutual, ambient treachery; the ordinary weather of this court.
- **笑里藏刀** (*xiào lǐ cáng dāo*) — "a knife hidden in a smile." Li Yifu's epitaph, effectively.
- **口蜜腹剑** (*kǒu mì fù jiàn*) — "honey mouth, sword belly." Li Linfu's.

## Frequently asked

**What dynasty is Blossoms of Power set in?**
None — it's 架空 (jiàkōng), a fictional, deliberately un-dated dynasty.

**Is Blossoms of Power based on real history?**
No. But its title comes from a real late-Tang poem by the rebel Huang Chao, and its court furniture is Tang.

**What does 架空 mean?**
"Suspended in mid-air" — a fictional setting that borrows a period's look and institutions without claiming to be it.

**Is the incense culture real?**
Yes. 香道, the way of incense, is a genuine scholar-elite discipline, codified in the Song as one of the "four idle arts."

**What is a 郡主?**
A commandery princess — a specific rank of imperial noblewoman, below 公主 (an emperor's daughter). Shen Xihe holds it as 昭宁郡主.

**Is it connected to any real emperor?**
No. The emperors, the court and the geography are invented.
