**Quick answer:** *Blossoms of Power* (**百花杀**, *Bǎi Huā Shā*) has a **happy ending** — and a deliberately gentle one, softer than the source novel. Shen Xihe (**沈汐和**, *Shěn Xīhé*) and Xiao Huayong (**萧华雍**, *Xiāo Huáyōng*) **end up together.** The finale puts Xiao Huayong through poisoning and an apparent death, and for a stretch the drama plays it as a genuine tragedy — but he survives, having faked the death as the last move of the endgame. After a time skip, the two reunite as a family with their young son and **govern side by side as equals** rather than as protector and protected. Shen Xihe finishes the series not as an avenger but as a reformer, building the fragrance bureau into a real institution and pulling other women into office behind her. The drama ran **36 episodes** on Tencent Video, premiering July 9 and closing out in late July 2026.

*Full spoilers for the finale follow.*

## How the finale actually plays out

For most of its run, *Blossoms of Power* is a revenge engine. Shen Xihe enters the palace carrying a debt, and her weapon isn't a blade but a nose — the **香刃** (*xiāng rèn*), "scent as a blade," the poisons, perfumes and medicinal knowledge that let a woman with no army move against men who have several. (We unpacked that whole system in [the incense-culture piece](/blog/blossoms-of-power-incense-culture-scent-as-weapon).)

The endgame turns on a palace coup. Xiao Huayong is poisoned and gravely injured, and the drama commits to the bit: for a stretch of the finale, the audience is meant to believe the Crown Prince is dead and that Shen Xihe has won her war and lost the person she wanted to win it with.

He isn't dead. The apparent death is the last feint in a series built on feints — the survival is revealed after a time skip, and the reunion is the emotional payload of the last episode.

## Do Shen Xihe and Xiao Huayong end up together?

Yes — and the shape of it is the point.

The reunion isn't a rescue. When the drama picks them back up after the time skip, they're a family of three with a young son, and critically, they are **governing together**. Chinese reviewers singled this out: the ending refuses the standard palace-drama landing where the heroine's reward is being kept safe by a powerful man. Shen Xihe doesn't retire into a consort role. The two are framed as co-rulers — partners in the work, not one leaning on the other.

That's why the finale was described in Chinese coverage as **温柔圆满** (*wēn róu yuán mǎn*) — "gentle and complete." Not triumphant. Not bloody. Complete.

## The real ending is what Shen Xihe builds

The most interesting thing in the finale isn't the romance. It's the institution.

Having spent the series using fragrance and pharmacology as a private weapon, Shen Xihe **turns her craft into a bureau** — elevating the fragrance office into a genuine organ of state with authority over medicinal materials, military intelligence, and postal communication. It's a very deliberate move by the writers: the skill that was survival tooling for one woman becomes infrastructure that outlives her.

And she staffs it deliberately. The finale shows her promoting women out of hard, low-status backgrounds into official positions — 打破时代偏见, "breaking the prejudices of the era," as Chinese recaps put it. A revenge plot ends as a hiring policy, which is a stranger and better ending than the genre usually allows.

## How the title flips meaning

The title comes from a Tang rebel poem — Huang Chao's 《不第后赋菊》, written after he failed the imperial examinations:

> **待到秋来九月八，我花开后百花杀。**
> *Dài dào qiū lái jiǔ yuè bā, wǒ huā kāi hòu bǎi huā shā.*
> "Wait until autumn comes, the eighth of the ninth month — when my flower blooms, the hundred flowers die."

For 30-odd episodes, that reading holds. It's a threat. Huang Chao meant it as one, and Shen Xihe uses it as one: *my turn is coming, and when it does, the rest of you are finished.* (We took the poem apart properly in [the title-poem article](/blog/blossoms-of-power-title-poem-huang-chao-meaning).)

The ending reverses it. In the finale's framing, one flower blooming is no longer what *kills* the hundred flowers — it's what **makes them bloom**. 杀 (kill) gets quietly traded for 开 (bloom). The line stops being about eliminating rivals and becomes about being first through a door you then hold open.

It's the neatest thing the drama does. The words don't change; the woman saying them does.

## Who doesn't get a happy ending

Not everyone lands well. Chinese fandom's **意难平** (*yì nán píng*) for this drama — the character whose ending viewers couldn't make peace with — was **Xiao Changqing** (**萧长卿**, played by Xu Zhengxi). 意难平 is worth learning if you read Chinese drama discourse: literally "the feeling won't settle," it's the specific ache of a character who deserved better and didn't get it. Every big Chinese drama produces one, and the fandom argument over who it is *is* part of the viewing.

## How it differs from the novel

The adaptation is noticeably kinder than its source. The novel's ending carries a tragic, melancholic weight that the drama trades for warmth — the fake-death is resolved, the family is intact, the supporting cast gets landings. Book readers are split on it in the usual way: some call it a betrayal of the original's bleakness, others think the reform-and-rebuild ending is a better fit for what the drama actually became, which is a story about building something rather than burning something.

## The idioms behind the ending

Three chengyu carry the finale, and all three are in the drama's active vocabulary:

- **绝处逢生** (*jué chù féng shēng*) — "to find life in a place of certain death." The fake-death reveal, exactly.
- **化敌为友** (*huà dí wéi yǒu*) — "turn enemies into friends." The court Shen Xihe ends up governing is largely built from people she once had reason to destroy.
- **推己及人** (*tuī jǐ jí rén*) — "extend from yourself to others." The hiring policy in four characters: she was a woman with no path, so she builds paths.

## Frequently asked

**Does Xiao Huayong die?**
No. He is poisoned and appears to die during the coup, but the death is faked and revealed as a strategic move. He survives and reunites with Shen Xihe after the time skip.

**Is the ending happy or sad?**
Happy — and specifically described in Chinese coverage as "gentle and complete" (温柔圆满). There's a stretch of the finale engineered to feel tragic before it resolves.

**Do they have a child?**
Yes. The post-time-skip reunion shows them as a family of three.

**Is it worth watching if you know the ending?**
The pleasure of this one is the scent-craft plotting, not the ending twist. Knowing the leads survive costs you very little.
