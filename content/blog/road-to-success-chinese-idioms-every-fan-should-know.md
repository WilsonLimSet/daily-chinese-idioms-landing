*Road to Success* (**灿如繁星**) is a sports drama, a mystery, and — underneath both — an argument about teaching. That makes its idiom vocabulary unusually rich: comeback chengyu, team chengyu, and a pair of two-thousand-year-old Confucian lines about who deserves an education.

Here are the eleven worth knowing, matched to where they belong in the story.

---

## The one in the title

### 狭路相逢 (xiá lù xiāng féng)
**"To meet on a narrow road."**

The source novel is called **《狭路》** for this idiom. It describes an encounter where **neither side can step aside** — a path too tight to pass on, so someone yields or you collide. The classical line finishes **勇者胜** (*yǒng zhě shèng*), "the brave one wins."

It's the drama's setup in four characters. A disgraced academic, a coach hiding from his own guilt, and a squad of kids nobody wanted are forced onto the same narrow path with no way around each other. ([More on the two titles →](/blog/road-to-success-chinese-name-character-names-explained))

## The teaching idioms — the drama's actual thesis

### [有教无类 (yǒu jiào wú lèi)](/blog/you-jiao-wu-lei)
**"In education, there are no classes of people."**

Confucius, *Analects* 15.39, and arguably the most important sentence in the Chinese educational tradition. It means teaching should be offered **without regard to a student's background** — rich or poor, gifted or slow, well-born or not.

This is the whole drama. Hongjing No. 8's team is built from **问题少年** (*wèntí shàonián*), "problem kids" — students the academic track had already sorted out and given up on. The drama's position is that "problem kid" describes a situation, not a person, which is 有教无类 restated in modern terms.

### [因材施教 (yīn cái shī jiào)](/blog/yin-cai-shi-jiao)
**"Teach according to the material."**

The companion principle: adapt your method to the individual student rather than running everyone through the same mould. Also Confucian, and the thing a good coach actually does — which is why Wang Fa, a man trained at the elite end of world football, has to completely rebuild his approach for a team of teenagers who have been told they're worthless.

Put the two together and you have the drama's argument: **everyone gets taught (有教无类), and each gets taught differently (因材施教).**

## The comeback idioms

### [东山再起 (dōng shān zài qǐ)](/blog/dong-shan-zai-qi)
**"To rise again from the eastern mountain."**

To stage a comeback after being out of the game. From Xie An, the Jin-dynasty statesman who withdrew to the Eastern Mountains and was later recalled to save the state. It applies to all three parties at once — Lin Wanxing's academic career, Wang Fa's coaching, and a football team that had been disbanded outright.

### [卧薪尝胆 (wò xīn cháng dǎn)](/blog/wo-xin-chang-dan)
**"To sleep on brushwood and taste gall."**

King Goujian of Yue, defeated and humiliated, slept on firewood and tasted bile daily so he'd never forget what he owed. It means enduring deliberate hardship to fuel an eventual reckoning.

The drama's version is quieter and more humiliating: a psychology PhD handing out balls and bibs as a **sports equipment custodian**, a job placed far beneath her credentials. That's her brushwood.

### [苦尽甘来 (kǔ jìn gān lái)](/blog/ku-jin-gan-lai)
**"Bitterness ends, sweetness comes."**

The finale in four characters. ([Ending explained →](/blog/road-to-success-ending-explained-happy-or-sad-lin-wanxing-wang-fa))

## The perseverance idioms

### [锲而不舍 (qiè ér bù shě)](/blog/qie-er-bu-she)
**"To keep carving and never let go."**

From Xunzi: carve without stopping and even metal and stone will yield. The training-montage idiom, and the one Chinese teachers write on classroom walls.

### [百折不挠 (bǎi zhé bù náo)](/blog/bai-zhe-bu-nao)
**"Bent a hundred times, never broken."**

Setback-proof resilience. Where 锲而不舍 is about persistence over time, 百折不挠 is about not deforming under repeated blows — the right one for a team that loses before it wins.

## The team idioms

### [众志成城 (zhòng zhì chéng chéng)](/blog/zhong-zhi-cheng-cheng)
**"When many wills unite, they form a fortress."**

The clearest expression in Chinese of a group becoming stronger than its members — and the direct chengyu cousin of the title. 灿如繁星 is the poetic version of the same idea: many small lights make a sky.

### [同舟共济 (tóng zhōu gòng jì)](/blog/tong-zhou-gong-ji)
**"In the same boat, crossing together."**

From Sunzi: even old enemies in one boat in a storm will cooperate. The locker-room idiom, for a squad who mostly can't stand each other at the start.

### [破釜沉舟 (pò fǔ chén zhōu)](/blog/po-fu-chen-zhou)
**"Smash the cauldrons, sink the boats."**

Xiang Yu destroyed his army's cookware and ships so retreat was impossible. Total commitment with the exit removed — the knockout-match idiom.

### [孤注一掷 (gū zhù yī zhì)](/blog/gu-zhu-yi-zhi)
**"Stake everything on a single throw."**

Its darker twin. 破釜沉舟 is committed; **孤注一掷 is reckless** — a gambler pushing in the last chip. Chinese commentary uses it for a desperate final gamble, and the distinction between the two is one of the more useful things a learner can pick up: *removing your own retreat* versus *betting everything on one roll*.
